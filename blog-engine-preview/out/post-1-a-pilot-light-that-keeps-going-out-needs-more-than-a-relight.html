A Pilot Light That Keeps Going Out Needs More Than a Relight

If your water heater pilot light keeps going out, relighting it may get you hot water for a few hours or days, but it does not fix the reason the flame is shutting off. On a gas water heater, the pilot has to heat a safety sensor continuously. If that sensor is dirty, worn out, out of position, or the pilot flame is weak, the gas control shuts the burner down instead of allowing unburned gas into the room.

Do not keep trying to relight the pilot if you smell gas near the heater. Leave the area, avoid operating light switches or appliances nearby, and call the gas utility or emergency services from outside. A faint gas smell, a hissing sound, or a pilot flame that flares up and dies repeatedly is not a problem to troubleshoot with matches.

Start with the pilot flame you can see

Take off the outer access panel if your heater has one, then look through the small viewing window while the pilot is lit. A healthy pilot is usually a steady blue flame, often with a small yellow tip, and it should wrap around the top 3/8 to 1/2 inch of the thermocouple. The thermocouple is the narrow copper-colored probe sitting directly in the pilot flame. If the flame barely touches it, blows sideways, looks mostly yellow, or pulses and sputters, the sensor cannot stay hot enough to keep the gas valve open.

Dust, lint, pet hair, and scale can partly block the pilot orifice, which is the tiny opening that feeds gas to the pilot burner. This is common when the water heater sits in a garage, utility closet, or laundry area. You may also see dust packed around the combustion-air screen near the bottom of the tank. That screen needs open airflow; a clogged screen can make the pilot and main burner starve for air.

A homeowner can check the area around the heater today without tools. Move stored boxes, paint cans, laundry baskets, and other items at least a few feet away from the base of the tank. Then check that the room does not feel unusually stuffy and that no return-air grille or dryer vent is blowing directly toward the water heater. Strong drafts can disturb a pilot flame, especially on an older atmospheric-vent heater.

The usual failed parts are small, but the diagnosis matters

On many standing-pilot heaters, the thermocouple is the part that fails first. It creates a small electrical signal from heat. Once it weakens, it may hold the pilot only while you keep the control knob pressed down, then the flame goes out as soon as you release it. A thermocouple often lasts around 5 to 10 years, though a dirty flame or repeated overheating can shorten that life.

Some heaters use a thermopile instead of a basic thermocouple. A thermopile is larger and produces enough power to operate the gas valve and, on certain models, an electronic status light. If your heater has a blinking indicator light, count the flashes before resetting anything. The flash pattern can point to a low-temperature limit switch, a vapor sensor issue, a combustion-air restriction, or a gas control fault. Resetting the heater without finding the cause can leave you with the same shutdown again the next morning.

The gas control valve can also fail. This is the dial assembly where you select OFF, PILOT, and a temperature setting. If the pilot flame looks strong and properly covers the sensor but still drops out, the control may not be holding the safety circuit open. A plumber can test the sensor output, inspect the pilot tube and burner assembly, and determine whether replacing a small sensor will solve the problem or whether the gas control is the actual failure.

Venting and water heater age can change the repair decision

An atmospheric gas water heater sends combustion gases up through the draft hood on top of the tank and into the metal vent pipe. If that vent has come loose, is visibly crushed, or if the draft hood is covered by stored items, exhaust can spill back around the heater. This can affect combustion and may cause pilot trouble. Do not tape, alter, or disconnect the vent yourself. If you see a separated vent joint or dark soot marks above the heater, turn the control to OFF and have it checked.

Look at the manufacturer label for the installation date or serial-number date. A tank that is 12 years old, has rust around the bottom seam, or leaves a small puddle underneath may not be a good candidate for putting money into a gas valve and burner repair. A leak from a pipe fitting can sometimes be repaired; water seeping from the tank body itself means the tank is failing.

Sediment at the bottom of the tank can make the burner run longer, create popping or rumbling sounds, and reduce how much usable hot water you get. The hard water effects on a water heater can show up as lukewarm water even when the pilot is staying lit.

Set the temperature dial back to its normal setting after any relight attempt; many homes use about 120°F to reduce scald risk while still providing usable hot water. If the pilot goes out again within 24 hours, the flame is yellow or unstable, the status light reports a fault, or you have to hold the pilot button down longer than the normal 30 to 60 seconds, schedule a diagnosis rather than continuing to relight it.
